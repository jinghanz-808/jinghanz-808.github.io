About

I am a Computer Science undergraduate at the University of Utah. I work in the IoTrust Lab on self-supervised and multimodal representation learning for egocentric video, IMU, and neural data collected in real-world environments.

When I began working on real-world navigation research, I focused on using machine learning to identify meaningful events in continuous video. As the project went on, I became more interested in what these events could help us understand about human behavior and brain activity. A doorway crossing, for example, may involve turning, changes in lighting, and entering a different environment at nearly the same moment. This made me think more carefully about how the data we collect shape the questions we can answer.

This project has helped me understand why I want to pursue a PhD. Research can be frustrating, especially when I spend a lot of time on an analysis and still cannot answer the main question. But I find it rewarding to see that question become clearer as I work on it. Looking back, I can see how an earlier attempt led me to reconsider a hypothesis or try a different comparison. Even when I do not have an answer yet, I can understand how my thinking has changed and what I need to examine next.

My broader research goal is to connect model evaluation with decisions about data collection. I am interested in systems that bring together environmental conditions, human behavior, and sensor measurements to examine what a model has learned and where its conclusions remain uncertain. Those limitations could help identify which conditions or measurements to collect next.

Research

I work on the Neural Science in the Wild project. It studies human behavior and brain activity during real-world navigation.

Events in egocentric video

My initial work mainly focused on identifying events in continuous first-person video. I reviewed the research behind the event categories and developed tools to compare when events were marked across annotation sources. I then used FINCH, an unsupervised clustering method, to group video features and compare the resulting boundaries with the annotated events. I wanted to understand what each source captured and how we could use it to answer our research question.

Brain activity during navigation

More recently, I have started examining intracranial EEG recordings alongside navigation events. I also examined turns away from doorways to explore whether changes near a doorway might be related to the turn itself. These comparisons helped me see what the existing data could not tell us. When movement, environmental changes, and navigation demands repeatedly occur together, more analysis may still not be enough to distinguish their contributions.

Adaptive data collection

I began considering what additional comparisons and measurements we would need to study them separately. I plan to combine video and movement measurements with brain recordings and behavioral annotations to compare navigation performance under similar movement conditions. Where the data lack suitable comparisons, I would use the analysis to select additional routes or tasks that provide them.
