About

I am a Computer Science undergraduate at the University of Utah, where I work in the IoTrust Lab on self-supervised and multimodal representation learning for egocentric video, IMU, and neural data collected in real-world environments.

I began by using machine learning to identify meaningful events in continuous first-person video. As the project developed, I became more interested in what those events can tell us about human behavior and brain activity. A doorway crossing, for example, can involve turning, a change in lighting, and entering a different environment at nearly the same moment — which raises a careful question about what our data can and cannot distinguish.

That question now guides my work: I want to connect model evaluation with decisions about data collection, so that what a model has learned — and where it remains uncertain — helps us choose the conditions, comparisons, and measurements to collect next.

I am applying to PhD programs in Computer Science to continue this work, with a focus on machine learning and sensing systems for real-world navigation.

Research

I work on the Neural Science in the Wild project, studying human behavior and brain activity during real-world navigation.

Events in egocentric video

Comparing annotated events with unsupervised boundaries from video features, including FINCH clustering and EgoVLP features. Annotated turns and clustering boundaries sometimes agree, but agreement alone does not mean they identify the same events — so I examine what each source captures and how it can answer our research question.

Brain activity during navigation

Examining intracranial EEG (iEEG) alongside navigation events. I have observed power decreases around turns, navigation errors, and corrections, and I study how those changes relate to movement, memory, and environmental transitions — including turns at junctions and crossings through doorways.

Adaptive data collection

Building an automated pipeline that turns manual checks into repeatable evidence checks: separate time anchors for when a door is seen, fixated, and crossed; control for head turns and overlapping events; and check whether effects hold across patients and walks. The goal is to use current evidence to design the next round of data collection.

Selected findings

  • Turns as event boundaries: anterior hippocampal theta, alpha, and beta activity decreases around perceived event boundaries, while theta (4–8 Hz) increases during event periods.
  • Errors matter: incorrect turns show a larger pre-to-post power decrease than correct turns in 4 of 5 patients, and power also drops around corrections, whether by the patient or by others.
  • Doorways have three times: a door can be seen, fixated, and crossed seconds apart. In one example, those times were −7.5 s, −7.1 s, and +1.0 s relative to the doorway label, and 33% of doors were first seen during a head turn.
  • Effects are not always general: a pooled Lost End effect is driven largely by one patient, which is why I report per-patient contributions and leave-one-out checks before drawing conclusions.

Findings are preliminary analyses from ongoing work. Aggregate results are reported here; no individual patient data are shared.
